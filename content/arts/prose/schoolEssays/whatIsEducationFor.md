We begin with the question of what is education for? To accurately and precisely answer it however, we must define it a bit further and set some constraints. I’ll begin with this objective truth: everything humans as a species do is in pursuit of some level of self-preservation and expansion. This fundamental behavior is evident at least at the individual level if not at the communal or societal. Even a belligerent nation’s leader, intent on world war, is acting in the interest of defending their nation or enriching friends in the defense industry. Either way, both of these are acts of self preservation whether it be on the individual or communal level. So if everything we do is for the sake of self preservation and expansion, what role does education play in that? This is the question we must answer: what role does education play in the expansion and preservation of the human species (on an individual and societal level)? Preservation meaning the sustenance of life and expansion meaning the progression of technology, the baseline of unilateral livelihood, and individual fulfillment/happiness. Before we continue, though, let’s set some constraints. What is education? For the purposes of this discussion, it's a piece of societal infrastructure that systematically instills mindsets and teaches information to a large group of people. The most effective information and mindsets to teach would be knowledge, morals/cultural norms, and dispositions. These 3 are the mechanisms education teaches to further society. They’re the specific intellectual and behavioral teachings required for the effective individual human function in modern society on any level. Because these teachings are directly tied to our survival (and flourishment), their compounding would ensure the continued preservation and expansion of humanity. The way these work together is that knowledge serves as the raw base of information for an individual. Dispositions are the various cultivated mindsets and skills within an individual. These make the garnering of information more efficient (for an individual) and the application of the information outwardly more effective (for society). The social norms are the final framework that guide and filter the implementation of knowledge, ensuring it is indeed being applied in the direction of compounding societal progress. Evidently, education is our means of achieving extragenic evolution (evolution beyond genetics). Its purpose is to compound knowledge in the individual as the theoretical base and implement frameworks of individual dispositions and societal norms as tools to garner and apply that raw knowledge on a societal scale. 
	
The education system must provide a lossless transfer of the theoretical base: knowledge. Otherwise, our species will be forced into a cycle of rediscovering what past generations already knew, halting the momentum of progress. We require this floor; generational efforts shouldn’t be squandered in restoring that which the past generation already had. For example, today, every individual doesn't have to derive the laws of electromagnetism and conduct experiments to discover the electron just to build a new electronic circuit. We already have that base of knowledge established. It's systematically taught to us so we can focus on pushing beyond.  However, the way in which knowledge is currently transferred is incredibly flawed. Cathy Davidosn addresses this in her book, “The New Education” which traces this issue back to its roots:  “The revolution in higher education was partly inspired by the management theorists of the day who were gauging the productivity of factories smelting pig iron and assembly lines turning out Model Ts (Davidson 5).” Higher education was originally modeled after the productivity of factories and assembly lines which worked in a system built for the 20th century, but not the one we live in today. The system teaches knowledge in isolation and without application, focusing on what students can regurgitate instead of how it can actually be applied. Davidson argues that “students need new ways of integrating knowledge…they need to be offered challenges that promote their success after graduation, when all the educational testing has stopped (Davidson 7).” This is exactly the point. Knowledge transfer must be interdisciplinary. It must be focused on the goal of application. She says the programs we must implement are the ones that “span undergraduate and professional education” and can cover the wide range of complex skills needed for the real world. Therefore, to ensure that human effort is always put towards expansion rather than stagnation, the transfer of knowledge must be as efficient as possible, ensuring that the progress of the previous generation becomes the baseline for the next.

Without being taught dispositions like creativity, perseverance, and adaptability much output will be left on the table in terms of taking in and applying knowledge. Schools must cultivate these mindsets and soft skills to ensure that students are absorbing the largest amount of knowledge possible, as efficiently as possible, and are then able to leverage it for discovery and output in society, whether it be in a research or a vocational setting. However when dispositions like creativity are neglected, inert knowledge stays inert. This is what Sir Ken Robsinon argues in the TED Talk, “Do Schools Kill Creativity?" He details how creativity is as important as literacy and how the current education system works against this. He also says “we don’t grow into creativity, we grow out of it (Robinson),” educated out of us by a system that has stigmatized our mistakes and our original thinking in favor of standardized output. And because of this, he says, “the consequence is that many highly-talented, brilliant, creative people think they're not, because the thing they were good at at school wasn't valued, or was actually stigmatized (Robinson)” Many individuals have the ability to apply knowledge in incredible ways but they’ve been wronged by the system and now lack the framework necessary to apply it in society. They may lack the creativity or drive or persistence in face of failure to do so. It's these qualities that allowed visionaries like Steve Jobs and Einstein to transform their inert knowledge into discoveries and products that moved entire industries (and society) forward. Without dispositions like these, an individual’s knowledge is wasted, neither efficiently cultivated nor transmuted into action that could make an impact beyond themself.

When education fails to instill a sense of morality, civic knowledge, and social norms, individual knowledge cannot be applied on the societal scale without causing dysfunction and deterring progress. Danielle Allen, the author of “What Is Education For?” addresses these points and is correct in emphasizing civic education but fails to see that civic education isn’t the end goal itself but it’s the societal filter through which knowledge should be responsibly applied. She states on page 11, “An education that prepares every student for civic and political engagement not only supports political equality but may also lead to increased economic fairness (Allen 11).“ This is because civic understanding creates the governmental and societal framework necessary for knowledge to actually be applied equitably and at the societal scale. Without it, knowledge would stagnate within individuals, and be suppressed by inequality, political repression, as well as the absence of functioning infrastructure. Without civic education, we wouldn’t be able to establish an efficient government that facilitates the application of knowledge and fights inequality. Allen reinforces this, stating that the liberal arts cultivates capacities for “ethical reasoning and social diagnosis (Allen 13).” These are the tools that empower individuals to identify the flaws of the society that surround them and take the necessary action to correct them. A great example of this is the inventor of the Polio Vaccine, Jonas Salk. He possessed the knowledge to cure polio but it was his moral compass that led to him releasing the vaccine freely rather than patenting it, a decision despite the opportunity for immense financial gain. His individual knowledge was transformed into an enormous societal good. Without proper civic and moral education filtering how knowledge is applied, it would inevitably serve the individual rather than compound humanity’s progress.

The ideal purpose of education is to expand upon the progress of the previous generations. To do this, it must instill in students a pool of raw information as well as the individual mental frameworks to efficiently absorb it, and the societal civic understanding to responsibly apply this knowledge. These three aspects are not independent, however. Knowledge without civic norms produces individuals who apply their capabilities for pure self-interest and lack any obligation for societal responsibility. Companies like Palantir are great examples where brilliant individuals have little regard for the civic and ethical consequences of their creations. Knowledge without dispositions leaves more knowledge to be gained on the table and the knowledge that has been gained much more susceptible to being squandered. Civic understanding without knowledge may yield a functional society that seeks to improve but lacks any actual knowledge to sustain itself, let alone propel it forward. All three of these must be present and balanced, each one course-correcting the excesses of the others. They formulaically come together and ensure that students are prepared and productive in their era’s society. The ideas that Allen and Robinson bring up regarding civic engagement and creative disposition as the goals of education are not mutually exclusive ideas. In fact, they're both two sides of the same coin; both visions for the garnering and application of knowledge at the individual and societal level. And Davidson’s push for interdisciplinary, application driven learning is simply the vessel through which knowledge is most efficiently transferred and taught. All of these ideas are nested under the same umbrella, all working towards one larger, common goal. Until we collectively recognize and agree upon what education is really for, we will always be pulled in too many directions, our intentions muddled and us stuck building systems that serve fragments of the goal rather than the whole. Without remedying this, humanity’s progress will never reach what it could be.
