## My Gravitational Field

Throughout my life I've found myself interested in a lot of different things, but a few have really capture my interest: emergent complexity, abstraction and representation, and systems.

I've always had difficulty picking a field I want to work in because I've realized that it's less about the field itself and more about the nature of the work. I enjoy difficult things: systems I have to pull apart, ideas I don't yet understand, or something new I have the chance to build.

## Between Abstraction and Reality

As such, I've gravitated to some larger disciplines, those being: physics, math, and computer science. Although, I enjoy these subjects individually, there is something very natural/poetic about how they combine together. It's easiest to explain this by walking you through the connection from the beginning (at least the way I see it):

I enjoy reducing systems to their primitives, rules, and constraints: finding a representation for something, prescribing an architecture or rule system, and then building something new with that structure.

Mathematics grants those abstractions structure. It gives me a language for defining relationships precisely, reasoning about their consequences, and discovering properties that aren't native to any individual piece.

Computation turns that structure into a process. Instead of just designing a system, I can bestow life upon it: watch behavior bloom, modify its architecture, and witness the consequences.

Physics grounds that abstraction in reality. It gives us mathematical models of physical systems, while also imposing the constraints that those models must ultimately answer to. Hardware lies at the boundary of all of this, where theoretical computation is realized through a physical substrate.

That's largely why I chose Computer Engineering: because it lets me keep exploring all of these without neccesarily committing myself entirely to just one.

This curiosity eventually led me to questions in computational complexity, particularly P vs. NP, which made me think much more seriously about the limits of computation. I was pushed beyond simply figuring out how to write a better program to understanding how representation, algorithms, architecture, and the model of computation itself determine which problems are tractable.

That's what makes alternative computational architectures so interesting to me. A change at any layer (from representation and algorithm to architecture or physical substrate) can create a very different space of capabilities and constraints, a space that has endless implications and applications.

## How I Like to Work

I've talked a lot about what interests me, but less about how I like to work. Simply put, I like building and modifying things to understand them. 

I enjoy work where implementation and theory feed back into one another: where I build something, discover that the abstraction was wrong or incomplete, and return to the theory with a better understanding. The projects I enjoy most working on grant me the freedom to follow this wherever it goes. 

My appreciation for that process grew through materials research. I've had the opportunity to work in both university and industry research environments, including work on ceramic-polymer composite materials. I came to really enjoy situations where the destination isn't fully known and to progress you're required to experiment, adjust and gradually figure out what the problem is asking of you.
That uncertainty is now one of the things I find most appealing about research. It's a whole space left to explore, and somewhere within it may lie something entirely new.

Becoming an Eagle Scout influenced another side of how I work. I learned that I really enjoy ownership: being handed a large problem with lots of constraints and moving pieces and having to figure out how to get it done. Some of the experiences I've valued most have been things that initially felt far outside my ability or comfort zone, where I had no choice but to learn, adapt, and keep going.
It's very exciting to be faced with a metaphorical (and literal) mountain to climb.

Between research, personal projects, and everything else I've done, I've found that I do my best work when I can combine curiosity with ownership. It's here I can really understand a problem deeply and make decisions about its direction, building something concrete and having the chance to learn from what happens.

## The GOATs

To spare you some of my rambling, I will keep these a bit shorter: 

Some of my largest inspirations up to this point in my life have been Richard Feynman, Steve Jobs, and Kim Jung Gi. 

These three near-perfectly sum up exactly what I aspire to achieve in all aspects of my life: 
1. For me **Richard Feynman** (Father of Quantam Electrodynamics) stands for the kind of scientific curiosity I aspire to maintain. I admire the way he was driven by wanting to truly understand things, and the groundbreaking work that came from following that curiosity. He reminds me to keep learning, keep asking questions, and hopefully never lose the joy of figuring things out.
2. **Steve Jobs** (Co-founder of Apple) stood for innovation and served to bring together design and engineering. He was a great thinker that sought to innovate and never stopped short of greatness. I've begun to value taste and art in all of my projects, engineering related and otherwise, and one day put my heart and soul into building something great. 
3. **Kim Jung Gi** (Artist/Illustrator) represents mastery of craft to me. The command he developed over the pen after decades of relentless practice is INCREDIBLE. His work reminds me how far a skill can be pushed and motivates me to keep developing my own abilities with that same seriousness and persistence.

## What Lies Ahead is Uncertain...

Given all of this, I'm not too set on any particular direction. I am interested in these things and a certain way of practicing them and would like to work on things that have large implications. So after my bachelors, this may mean pursuing a PhD, an R&D lab, or something I build myself. I care much more about continuing to put myself around difficult questions, capable people, and enough freedom to learn and create.


Given all of this, I'm intentionally not too set on a particular destination. I know the kinds of problems and environments that I enjoy much better than I know of the exact job I will eventually want. That might mean pursuing a PhD, joining an R&D lab or some other technical team, or building something of my own. 

Whatever it may be, I care much more about continuing to put myself around difficult questions, capable people, and enough freedom to learn and create.

I'm excited for whatever may lie ahead.
