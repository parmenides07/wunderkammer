## P vs. NP

The idea that has fascinated me most so far is the P vs. NP problem.

In simple terms, **P** refers to problems that computers can solve efficiently. **NP** refers to problems where a possible answer can be checked efficiently, even if finding that answer may be incredibly difficult.

P vs. NP asks whether every problem whose answer can be checked efficiently can also be solved efficiently.

The hardest problems in NP are called NP-complete problems. These include problems such as finding the most valuable combination of items that can fit inside a suitcase, determining the shortest route between many locations, creating the most efficient schedule, or finding the best arrangement within an enormous engineering design space.

As these problems grow, the number of possible answers becomes far too large for a normal computer to search through directly. Being able to solve them more efficiently could transform logistics, chip design, scientific discovery, materials design, scheduling, optimization, and many other fields.

No current technology is known to efficiently solve every NP-complete problem, but even getting meaningfully closer would open up a whole new world of developments and possibilities.

## New Forms of Computing

This is what led me toward alternate forms of computation such as quantum computing, neuromorphic computing, and artificial intelligence.

Quantum computing may allow us to approach certain problems through completely different computational rules. It does not simply test every possibility and immediately reveal the correct answer, but quantum algorithms can use interference and mathematical structure to search or calculate more efficiently in certain cases.

Neuromorphic computing and AI may approach difficult problems in another way. Rather than blindly checking every possibility, they may use learned intuition, heuristics, physical dynamics, parallelism, and better representations to narrow down which possibilities are worth exploring.

What fascinates me is not only the potential of these technologies, but also the fact that their systems are not completely settled. Researchers still need to define the algorithms, computational primitives, architectures, and “software” that allow them to function.

That is the kind of work I want to do.

I would ideally work around the line between hardware and software, probably leaning closer to algorithms, architecture, and computational models. I want to help push computation toward its next frontier by developing systems that make currently unreachable problems more tractable.

My current path is to continue studying Computer Engineering while minoring in Mathematics, explore quantum computing, neuromorphic computing, AI, and computational complexity, and eventually pursue a PhD in whichever area continues to interest me once I understand its actual technical details.

Afterward, I want to bring that work into industry or R&D, help turn foundational ideas into real technologies, and eventually contribute to defining and leading the development of new computational systems.
